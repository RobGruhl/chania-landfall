## Stop 1 — Souda Bay (harbour, fortress island, war cemetery, NATO)

**Facts**
1. Souda Bay is about 15 km long and is usually described as one of the largest and deepest natural harbours in the Mediterranean, sheltered by the Akrotiri peninsula. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souda_Bay
2. Venice fortified the small island of Souda at the bay's mouth because it controlled the entrance to the anchorage. The main works are usually dated to the 1570s (begun c. 1570, around the time Cyprus fell). **Construction dates not confirmed this session.** [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souda_(island)
3. Chania fell in 1645, but Souda, Gramvousa and Spinalonga stayed Venetian. Venice kept these island fortresses even after the 1669 peace that gave up Candia. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souda_(island)
4. Souda finally fell to the Ottomans in 1715, during the Ottoman–Venetian War of 1714–1718: 70 years after Chania and 46 years after Candia. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souda_(island)
5. When Chania surrendered on 22 August 1645, the Venetian garrison and townspeople who chose to leave were evacuated unharmed to Souda. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Chania_(1645)
6. Under the Ottomans the island later "served as a refuge for Cretan insurgents". [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souda_(island)
7. On 26 March 1941 the heavy cruiser HMS *York*, anchored in Souda Bay, was crippled by Italian explosive motor boats of the Decima Flottiglia MAS and beached. The Luftwaffe later bombed the wreck. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_York_(90)
8. The *Tanais*, the ship that later carried Chania's Jews, had been sunk once already by the Luftwaffe in Souda Bay on 26 May 1941, then raised and repaired. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinking_of_the_SS_Tanais
9. The Battle of Crete ran from 20 May to 1 June 1941 (Operation *Merkur*), the first mainly airborne invasion in history. Its decisive point was Maleme airfield, west of Chania. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Crete
10. Souda Bay War Cemetery (CWGC), designed by Louis de Soissons, holds 1,564 burials. [medium; the CWGC leaflet is the primary source] https://www.cwgc.org/media/l3zfz2cz/cwgc-crete-suda-bay-en.pdf
11. Of the Second World War burials, 731 are identified and 776 are unidentified. The cemetery also holds 19 First World War burials. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souda_Bay_War_Cemetery
12. By nationality the graves are mostly British (862), then New Zealand (446) and Australia (197), with a few South African, Canadian and Indian burials. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souda_Bay_War_Cemetery
13. Today the bay hosts Hellenic Navy facilities, US Naval Support Activity Souda Bay and NATO's Marathi pier, along with the NATO Missile Firing Installation (NAMFI) on Akrotiri. **Exact founding dates not checked.** [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Support_Activity_Souda_Bay

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **The fortress that wouldn't fall:** a rock in the bay flying the Lion of St Mark for 70 years after the city behind it fell. Venice even kept it through the 1669 peace, as a foothold for retaking the island one day.
- **Frogmen in the night, 1941:** Italian one-man explosive boats slip past the booms and cripple a British heavy cruiser at anchor.
- **The cursed ship:** the *Tanais*, sunk and raised in this bay in 1941, goes down again in 1944 with Chania's Jews aboard.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **A refugee boatlift in 1645:** families walking out of a surrendered city with whatever they could carry, ferried across to Souda.
- **The cemetery as a roll call of an empire's households:** farm boys from Otago and Queensland lie beside Londoners. The headstone inscriptions were chosen by families.

## Stop 2 — The Municipal Market (Agora), 1911–1913

**Facts**
1. Building began in 1911. [high] https://chaniahistory.gr/en/municipal-market/
2. The market stands on the remains of the Venetian **Piatta Forma** bastion, on the line of the city's eastern walls. [high] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/monument/the-municipal-market/
3. The cross-shaped plan was modelled on the covered market of **Marseille**. [medium; several tourism sources agree] https://www.explorecrete.com/chania/EN-chania-01-municipal-market.html
4. There are four wings, each entrance facing a cardinal direction, and 76 shops grouped around the arms. [medium] https://chaniahistory.gr/en/municipal-market/
5. Prime Minister **Eleftherios Venizelos** opened it on **4 December 1913**, three days after the Greek flag went up at Firkas on 1 December. [high] https://chaniahistory.gr/en/municipal-market/
6. The wings were traditionally divided by trade: butchers, fishmongers, greengrocers, and cheese and herb sellers. **Exact wing assignments not checked.** [medium] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/monument/the-municipal-market/
7. To make room, part of the Venetian walls and the bastion were demolished. This was part of the early-20th-century clearing of the south and east walls as the city outgrew them. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_of_Chania
8. The market closed for a long restoration (from 2014). **Reopening date not confirmed this session.** [low] https://visitchania.gr/en/places-to-visit/municipal-market-of-chania/

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **Grocers built on the guns:** the market sits on the stump of a Venetian artillery bastion. The town's new shopping centre literally replaced the thing designed to keep the Turks out.
- **Three days after the flag:** Venizelos, the local boy, comes home as Greek Prime Minister to cut the ribbon of a European-style market. It was a statement that Chania was now a modern Greek city.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **One roof for every trade:** a single cruciform hall replaced scattered street markets. It's the place to talk about who sold what, the cheese wing, and how a household shopped in 1913.
- **A municipal project of a brand-new Greek town:** public hygiene, standard stalls and civic pride.

## Stop 3 — Kastelli Hill (Minoan Kydonia, Byzantine wall, Venetian Castello)

**Facts**
1. Kastelli has been inhabited since the Neolithic and was the centre of ancient **Kydonia**. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania
2. The Bronze Age name appears as ***ku-do-ni-ja*** in Linear B tablets from Knossos. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania
3. Linear B tablets were also found at Kastelli itself, in the Greek-Swedish excavations around Agia Aikaterini Square. This made Chania one of the few Linear B find-spots outside Knossos. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kydonia
4. The **"Master Impression"** sealing, found at Kastelli, shows a male figure standing over a multi-storey town by the sea. It is one of the best Minoan images of a city. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_Impression
5. Homer names the Kydonians twice. In *Odyssey* 3.292 Menelaus's ships are wrecked on Crete "where the Kydonians dwelt about the streams of Iardanos". *Odyssey* 19.176 lists Kydonians among Crete's peoples. [medium] https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Hom.+Od.+3.292
6. Herodotus (3.44, 3.59) says Samian exiles settled Kydonia c. 524 BC and were driven out by Aeginetans five years later. [medium] https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Hdt.+3.59
7. The Romans conquered Crete in **69 BC** under Q. Caecilius Metellus. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania
8. After the Byzantine reconquest of 961, the Byzantines built a fortress on Kastelli in the **10th century** to keep out a second Arab invasion. Pieces of the wall survive, including on **Sifaka Street**. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_of_Chania
9. In **1252** the Venetian colonisation wave founded *La Canea* "on the site of the long abandoned ancient city". [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania
10. The Venetian **Castello** (the walled upper town on Kastelli) held the Rector's palace, the Latin cathedral and the homes of the leading families. Ordinary townsfolk lived outside it until the new walls of 1536–1568 enclosed them. [medium] https://tour.chania.gr/en/
11. A Venetian coat of arms dated **1624** survives on a ruined building on **Lithinon Street**, the "Archives" building. [medium] https://chaniahistory.gr/
12. Kastelli was heavily bombed in May 1941. The empty lots left by the bombing are why the archaeological pits can be seen today. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kydonia

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **The Master Impression:** a 3,500-year-old "establishing shot" of a god or king towering over a sea-town. It might even be this hill.
- **Layer cake of conquerors:** Minoan walls, a Byzantine wall built against Arab raiders, a Venetian castle, a 1941 bomb crater. Every faction left a layer.
- **Homer's shipwreck:** Menelaus's fleet smashed on the Kydonian coast on the way home from Troy.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **A castle town for the elite:** inside the Castello lived the Venetian nobles and officials; outside, Greek artisans and fishermen. Here the city's class geography is physical.
- **Tablets as household accounts:** Linear B is mostly bookkeeping (sheep, wool, oil, rations): the paperwork of ordinary Bronze Age life.

## Stop 4 — Splantzia Square and Agios Nikolaos (minaret plus bell tower)

**Facts**
1. Agios Nikolaos was built around **1320** by the Dominicans (the brotherhood of Candia) as a Roman Catholic monastery church. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Nicholas,_Chania
2. After 1645 the Ottomans converted it into the **Hünkar Camii** ("Sovereign's/Emperor's Mosque"), honouring **Sultan Ibrahim**, the sultan who launched the Cretan war. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Nicholas,_Chania
3. It was one of three churches turned into mosques straight after the fall of the city. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Chania_(1645)
4. The cylindrical minaret is about **36 m** tall. With its lost conical cap it would have topped **40 m**. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Nicholas,_Chania
5. It was reconsecrated as an Orthodox church and dedicated to St Nicholas in **1918**. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Nicholas,_Chania
6. The bell tower was added in the **1950s**, giving it both a minaret and a bell tower. Wikipedia calls it "the only building in Greece" with both. [medium; the claim is repeated by tourism sources, treat as "reportedly"] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Nicholas,_Chania
7. Splantzia (from Venetian *Piazza*) was the heart of the Ottoman Muslim quarter of Chania. Topanas, in the west, was the Christian quarter. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania
8. **Agioi Anargyroi**, a small church just off the square, is traditionally said to be the only church where Orthodox worship was allowed in Ottoman Chania. [low; tradition, check] https://www.west-crete.com/chania-splantzia.htm
9. The square's big plane tree is linked to the Ottoman-era hanging of Orthodox clergy and rebels. The bishop of Kissamos, **Melchisedek Despotakis**, is said to have been hanged here in 1821. [low; local tradition, verify before printing] https://creteinsider.com/splantzia-neighborhood-chania-crete/
10. Ottoman Chania was mixed: Muslims (many of them Cretan converts who spoke Greek), Orthodox Christians, Jews in Evraiki, and later Egyptian and Western merchants and consuls. Most Cretan Muslims left in the 1923 population exchange under the Treaty of Lausanne. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan_Muslims

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **The building that changed sides three times:** Catholic friars' church, then the Sultan's mosque, then an Orthodox church. It still wears the minaret and the bell tower like two faction banners.
- **The hanging tree:** the plane tree as the place where the Pasha made examples of rebels. Tell it as a legend, flagged as such.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **Neighbours who spoke the same language:** Cretan Muslims and Christians shared Greek, food and music, and prayed in different buildings on the same square.
- **1923, the last call to prayer:** families who had lived here for generations were "exchanged" to Anatolia. Their houses were then taken over by Orthodox refugees from Asia Minor.

## Stop 5 — The Venetian shipyards (Neoria / Arsenali) and the Grand Arsenal

**Facts**
1. The first two Neoria were finished in **1526**. [high] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/monument/venetian-neoria/
2. Sixteen had been built by **1593**, and the 17th completed the south range in **1599**. [high] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/monument/venetian-neoria/
3. A few years later **five more**, the **Moro** shipyards, were built on the east side of the harbour. That makes about 22 in all. **Contested:** some sources say 23. [medium] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/monument/venetian-neoria/
4. Each was a vaulted hall about **50 m long, 9 m wide and 10 m high**. Arched openings in the side walls connected the halls, and the seaward end was open so galleys could be hauled straight in. [high] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/monument/venetian-neoria/
5. They were used to repair, maintain and over-winter the galleys of Venice's Cretan squadron, and to build them. [medium] https://tour.chania.gr/en/route1/venetian-arsenals/venetian-arsenals.html
6. A row of **7** vaulted halls survives together, plus one more further west, the **Grand Arsenal** (Megalo Arsenali). [high] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/monument/venetian-neoria/
7. The Grand Arsenal is now the **Centre of Mediterranean Architecture**. [high] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/monument/venetian-neoria/
8. When the city fell in 1645, the Ottomans captured **12 galleys** in the arsenal. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Chania_(1645)
9. One of the Moro Neoria now houses the Maritime Museum's reconstructed Minoan ship *Minoa* (see Stop 8). [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_Museum_of_Crete
10. Later uses included Ottoman and Egyptian military stores, and 20th-century warehouses and workshops. The upper storey of the Grand Arsenal was a 19th-century addition. **Not checked.** [low] https://tour.chania.gr/en/route1/venetian-arsenals/venetian-arsenals.html

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **A dragon's lair for galleys:** long vaulted caves where war galleys were dragged in for the winter, out of storm season and safe from corsairs.
- **The prize in the cave:** the Ottomans walk in in August 1645 and find 12 Venetian galleys sitting in the sheds.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **Work at the arsenal:** caulkers, carpenters, rope-makers and oarsmen. This was the town's biggest employer and the reason many families lived here.
- **Same building, new trade:** a war factory that became warehouses, then an architecture centre. A building that kept finding new work.

## Stop 6 — The Venetian harbour and the Kioutsouk Hasan Mosque (Yiali Tzamisi)

**Facts**
1. The mosque was built in **1645**, right after the conquest. It is usually called the oldest surviving Ottoman building on Crete. **Contested:** sources give 1645 or 1645–1649 for construction; this brief uses "built from 1645". [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%BC%C3%A7%C3%BCk_Hasan_Pasha_Mosque
2. It is named for **Küçük ("Little") Hasan Pasha**, given as the first Ottoman governor/commander of the city. [medium] https://chaniahistory.gr/en/giali-tzamisi/
3. Its other names are **Yiali Tzamisi** ("Seaside Mosque") and the **Mosque of the Janissaries**. [high] https://chaniahistory.gr/en/giali-tzamisi/
4. It was the first Muslim place of worship the Ottomans built on Crete. [medium] https://chaniahistory.gr/en/giali-tzamisi/
5. A large central hemispherical dome sits on four great arches, with a row of smaller domes over the porch. **Contested:** the count of small domes varies by source, 6 or 7. [medium] https://chaniahistory.gr/en/giali-tzamisi/
6. Tradition names an **Armenian architect**, who also built a similar mosque at Spaniako in Selinos. [low] https://chaniahistory.gr/en/giali-tzamisi/
7. The minaret was demolished in the 20th century, in the 1920s or by 1939 (sources differ). [low] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%BC%C3%A7%C3%BCk_Hasan_Pasha_Mosque
8. After the 1923 population exchange it served as a warehouse, the **Archaeological Museum**, and a Greek Tourism Organisation office. It now belongs to the Port Fund and is used for exhibitions. [high] https://chaniahistory.gr/en/giali-tzamisi/

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **The Janissaries' chapel:** the mosque of the Sultan's elite infantry garrison, planted on the quay where the Venetian galleys used to tie up. It was a victory banner made of stone.
- **The architect from far away:** an Armenian master builder working for an Ottoman pasha in a captured Venetian port.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **A building that kept getting reassigned:** mosque, warehouse, museum, tourist office, gallery. You can read a century of the town's priorities in what it was used for.
- **The harbour as a working street:** fishermen, porters, coffee houses and customs men around the mosque.

## Stop 7 — The lighthouse and the breakwater

**Facts**
1. Venice first built a lighthouse at the end of the breakwater in the late 16th century, usually dated **1595–1601**. [medium] https://www.cretanbeaches.com/en/lighthouses/chania-lighthouse
2. It fell into neglect after the Ottoman conquest of 1645. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania_Lighthouse
3. The rebuilt tower is credited to the **Egyptians** during Mehmet Ali's rule of Crete (1830–1840), usually dated **c. 1839**. That is why it is called the "Egyptian Lighthouse". [medium] https://www.cretanbeaches.com/en/lighthouses/chania-lighthouse
4. **Contested date:** the English Wikipedia instead says it was "rebuilt in 1864". Writers should say "rebuilt in the 19th century under Egyptian, then Ottoman, rule" or flag both dates. [low] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania_Lighthouse
5. The tower has three sections: an **octagonal base**, a **16-sided middle** and a **round top**. The shape and the internal stone stair made people compare it to a **minaret**. [medium] https://www.cretanbeaches.com/en/lighthouses/chania-lighthouse
6. **Contested height:** about **21 m** (tourism sources) or **26 m** (Wikipedia). [low] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania_Lighthouse
7. A fixed white light was installed in **May 1915**, making it an official lighthouse of the Greek service. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania_Lighthouse
8. It was damaged in the Second World War, refurbished in **2005–2006**, and listed as an archaeological monument in **1962**. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania_Lighthouse
9. Today's light flashes red every 2.5 s, with a range of about 7 nautical miles. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania_Lighthouse
10. Mehmet Ali's Egypt held Crete from **1830 to 1840**, its reward for helping the Sultan against the Greek revolution. The island went back to direct Ottoman rule in 1840. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_rule_in_Crete

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **A minaret for ships:** an Egyptian governor rebuilds the Venetian light so it looks like a prayer tower, then shells and bombs chip at it in 1941.
- **The walk to the end of the world:** the breakwater as the city's sea-wall. Galleys used to line up behind it, and today it is the best view back at the whole city.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **Ten years of Egyptian rule:** a short, odd chapter of tax reforms, public works and the lighthouse, which also stirred revolt.
- **The evening walk:** the breakwater stroll to the lighthouse is still a family ritual. Point out who walks it now.

## Stop 8 — Firkas fortress and the Maritime Museum of Crete

**Facts**
1. Firkas was built in **1620** to protect the harbour, next to the San Salvatore bastion at the north-west corner of the walls. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_of_Chania
2. Its name comes from the Turkish *firka*, "barracks". Under the Ottomans it was a barracks and prison. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_Museum_of_Crete
3. On **1 December 1913** the Greek flag was raised on Firkas, the formal ceremony of Crete's union with Greece. King Constantine and Venizelos attended. [high for the date and place; medium for who attended] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/museum/nautical-museum-of-crete/
4. The legal basis was the **Treaty of London (30 May 1913)**, under which the Ottoman Empire gave up its rights to Crete. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan_State
5. The **Maritime Museum of Crete** was founded in **1973** and sits at the entrance to Firkas. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_Museum_of_Crete
6. It has three permanent exhibitions: the marine environment, the **Battle of Crete** (1941), and ancient and traditional shipbuilding. [medium] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/museum/nautical-museum-of-crete/
7. ***Minoa***, a **17 m** working reconstruction of a Minoan ship, was built in 2001–2004 for the Athens 2004 Olympics. It was sailed and rowed to Piraeus and is now displayed in a **Moro Neorio**. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_Museum_of_Crete
8. The Minoa project is linked to the underwater archaeologist **Elpida Hadjidaki**. [low; check her exact role] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elpida_Hadjidaki
9. The Germans also used Firkas as a prison during the Second World War. [low] https://www.chaniatourism.gr/

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **The flag over the barracks:** 1 December 1913. The Turkish barracks of Firkas, at the end of a 70-year rebellion, gets a blue-and-white flag. It is the "end credits" stop of the tour.
- **Rowing a Bronze Age warship to Athens:** a crew of modern oarsmen rows a hand-built Minoan ship to Piraeus in 2004.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **What a sailor's family lived on:** the traditional boatbuilding galleries show caïques, nets and tools, and how a fishing family made a living.
- **A town's own war:** the 1941 gallery is about civilians as much as soldiers. Cretan villagers fought paratroopers with whatever they had.

## Stop 9 — Topanas and Evraiki: the Jewish quarter, Etz Hayyim and the *Tanais*

**Facts**
1. **Topanas** (from Turkish *top-hane*, "cannon house/arsenal") was the Venetian nobles' quarter and later the Christian quarter in Ottoman times. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania
2. **Evraiki** (Ovraiki), the Jewish quarter, lies in the streets around Kondylaki Street. A Jewish community lived here under the Venetians and the Ottomans. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etz_Hayyim_Synagogue
3. Etz Hayyim was originally built as a church in the Venetian period and became a synagogue in the **17th century**, after the Ottoman conquest. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etz_Hayyim_Synagogue
4. On **29 May 1944** the Germans rounded up the Jews of Chania, about **250–275 people**. **Contested:** Wikipedia also cites "276 arrested on May 20". [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinking_of_the_SS_Tanais
5. They were trucked to Heraklion and put aboard the ***Tanais***, along with Greek hostages and resistance prisoners and Italian prisoners of war. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinking_of_the_SS_Tanais
6. On **9 June 1944** the British submarine **HMS *Vivid*** fired four torpedoes at 2,400 yards. Two hit, and the ship **sank in about 12 seconds**, around 14 nautical miles north-west of the islet of Dia. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinking_of_the_SS_Tanais
7. Estimates of the dead run from **425 to 1,000**. About **265** were Cretan Jews; there were up to 400 Greek prisoners and 100–300 Italian prisoners. [high, given as a range] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinking_of_the_SS_Tanais
8. The *Vivid*'s crew did not know who was aboard. The sinking marks the end of Crete's Jewish community. [high] https://eurojewcong.org/news/communities-news/greece/greek-jewish-community-honours-the-memory-of-the-victims-of-the-tanais-steamship-sinking/
9. The ruined synagogue was restored in the late 1990s and reopened in **1999**. The restoration was led by **Nikos Stavroulakis**. [high for 1999; medium for Stavroulakis] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etz_Hayyim_Synagogue
10. Arson attacks on **5 and 16 January 2010**. The second fire destroyed about **2,500 rare books and manuscripts**. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etz_Hayyim_Synagogue
11. Today the congregation is mostly non-Jewish. It follows Sephardic customs and has written its own Haggadah. Queen Sofía of Spain and ex-King Constantine II visited unannounced in **March 2006**. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etz_Hayyim_Synagogue
12. Chania has a memorial to the *Tanais* victims, with a commemoration every June. [medium] https://www.haniotika-nea.gr/vithisi-tou-tanais/

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **Twelve seconds:** a British submarine, an Allied torpedo, a German-run ship. Nobody on the *Vivid* knew who was in the hold. This is a tragedy, not a battle story, and should be told plainly.
- **Fire and books, 2010:** the synagogue burned twice in eleven days, and the community rebuilt it again.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **A courtyard community:** a synagogue tucked into a lane, with a mikveh, a garden and families around it, Jewish life lived among Christian and Muslim neighbours for centuries.
- **The names on the wall:** the memorial plaque at Etz Hayyim lists the deported families by name. It is the quietest stop of the tour.
- **A synagogue kept alive by strangers:** today's mostly non-Jewish congregation looks after a house of prayer whose own community is gone.

## Stop 10 — Eleftherios Venizelos, the revolts and the Cretan State (1866–1913)

**Facts**
1. **Venizelos** was born on **23 August 1864** at **Mournies**, just outside Chania. He died in Paris on **18 March 1936** and is buried on **Akrotiri** (Profitis Ilias), overlooking the city. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleftherios_Venizelos
2. The **Great Cretan Revolt of 1866–1869** is remembered above all for the **Arkadi Monastery** (near Rethymno), 8–9 November 1866. Rather than surrender, the defenders blew up the powder store. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan_revolt_(1866%E2%80%931869)
3. Further risings followed in **1878** (the Halepa Pact gave Crete limited home rule), **1889** and **1895–1897**. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pact_of_Halepa
4. In **February 1897** Cretan insurgents raised the Greek flag on **Profitis Ilias** on Akrotiri. Foreign warships shelled them. The famous story says the flagpole was shot away and a fighter, **Spyros Kayales**, held the flag up with his own body, and the admirals stopped firing out of respect. [low as told: legend, widely repeated; the 1897 shelling itself is medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleftherios_Venizelos
5. The Great Powers (Britain, France, Russia, Italy) occupied Crete and set up the autonomous **Cretan State** in **1898**, with **Chania as its capital**. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania
6. **Prince George of Greece** became High Commissioner and landed in December 1898. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan_State
7. In **1905** Venizelos led the **Theriso revolt** against Prince George's rule. George resigned in 1906. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theriso_revolt
8. **Halepa**, the elegant district east of the old town, was the diplomats' and Prince George's quarter. The **Venizelos house** is there. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halepa
9. Venizelos went to Athens in **1910** and served as Prime Minister of Greece for a long run of terms. Union came on **1 December 1913**. [high for union; medium for 1910] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleftherios_Venizelos
10. Chania stayed the capital of Crete until **1971**, when it moved to Heraklion. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chania

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **The flag-bearer of Profitis Ilias:** a rebel standing in for a shot-away flagpole under naval gunfire. Tell it as a legend, but it's a great one, and Venizelos's grave is on the same hill.
- **Rebel turned statesman:** Venizelos goes from rifle-carrying insurgent in the Theriso hills (1905) to Prime Minister of Greece five years later. A real-life rogue-to-king arc.
- **Four Great Powers, one tiny state:** Britain, France, Russia and Italy each take a sector, with Chania as an international capital full of consuls and gunboats.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **A capital of consuls:** in Halepa's mansions, foreign families, a prince's court and local lawyers' households made a small cosmopolitan world.
- **Revolt as a family business:** villages emptied for each rising. Women and children sheltered in monasteries like Arkadi, and every family had a man "in the mountains".

## Stop 11 — The Venetian walls, the 1645 siege, and the Cretan War

**Facts**
1. Work on the new walls began in **1536** (Wikipedia; some sources say 1538, after Barbarossa's raids). The designer was **Michele Sanmicheli**, and the walls were finished in **1568**. [high for 1536–1568 and Sanmicheli; 1538 is also cited] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_of_Chania
2. The cost was **87,000 ducats**. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_of_Chania
3. Walls up to **20 m** high, a moat **60 m wide and 15 m deep**. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_of_Chania
4. The four corner bastions were **San Salvatore** (NW, also called Gritti), **San Dimitrio** (SW, also Schiavo/Lando), **Santa Lucia** (SE) and **Mocenigo** (NE, also Sabbionara). [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_of_Chania
5. There were three gates: **Porta San Salvatore** (west), **Porta Retimiota** (south, towards Rethymno) and **Porta Sabbionara** (east). [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_of_Chania
6. The Ottoman armada left Istanbul on **30 April 1645**, reached Chania on **23 June**, and laid siege from **27 June**. The city surrendered on **22 August 1645**, after **56 days**. [high; some sources say 54 or 57 days] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Chania_(1645)
7. The commander was **Silahdar Yusuf Pasha**, with about **50,000 men** (7,000 Janissaries, 14,000 sipahis, 3,000 sappers) and **416 ships**, some of them hired Dutch and English vessels. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Chania_(1645)
8. The Venetian defence was **about 12,000**, described as an "inadequately recruited militia". [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Chania_(1645)
9. **Blasio Zulian** held the islet fort of **Agioi Theodoroi** with **30 men**. Rather than surrender, he "set up mines on the fort, blowing himself up, his men, and the fortress to pieces". [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Chania_(1645)
10. Istanbul celebrated the capture with **three days of cannon fire**. A Christian relief force (Tuscan, Spanish, Papal, Maltese) arrived and failed in a counterattack on 1 October. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Chania_(1645)
11. Yusuf Pasha left a garrison of **8,000**. He was executed in Istanbul in early 1646 after falling out with the Grand Vizier. [high for the garrison; medium for the execution] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silahdar_Yusuf_Pasha
12. The wider **Cretan War (1645–1669)** centred on the **Siege of Candia** (Heraklion), 1648–1669: about **21 years**, often called one of the longest sieges in history. [high] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Candia
13. **Francesco Morosini** surrendered Candia in **September 1669** to Grand Vizier **Köprülü Fazıl Ahmed Pasha**. Venice kept only Souda, Gramvousa and Spinalonga. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Candia
14. The war was sparked in 1644 when the Knights of Malta captured an Ottoman ship carrying, among others, a member of the Sultan's harem, then put in at Crete. Sultan Ibrahim blamed Venice. [medium] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan_War_(1645%E2%80%931669)

**Story hooks — Rob**
- **Blasio Zulian's last stand:** 30 men, old cannon, an island fort, and a powder-store choice. It is Crete's Alamo.
- **The casus belli:** Maltese knights, a captured harem ship, and an enraged sultan. A pirate raid starts a 25-year war.
- **Siege math:** 50,000 men and 416 ships against 12,000 militia. Chania lasts 56 days; Candia, with the whole of Christendom's mercenaries, lasts 21 years.

**Story hooks — Jamie**
- **Militia, not knights:** the 12,000 defenders were mostly local Greek townsmen and peasants pressed into service for Venetian masters. Whose war was it?
- **After the fall:** churches became mosques within weeks, Venetian families shipped out, and Cretans negotiated life under new tax and religious rules. Many converted over the following generations.

## Contested points: a quick list for the writers
- **Lighthouse rebuild:** c. 1839 (Egyptian; most local sources) vs 1864 (en.wikipedia). Height 21 m vs 26 m.
- **Siege length:** 56 days (Wikipedia, 27 Jun – 22 Aug) vs 54 or 57 days elsewhere.
- **Wall start:** 1536 vs 1538.
- **Number of Neoria:** 17 south + 5 Moro = 22; some sources say 23.
- **Kioutsouk Hasan:** construction 1645 vs 1645–49; small domes 6 vs 7; date the minaret was demolished.
- **Tanais:** dead 425–1,000; Jews aboard ~265 (276 arrested is also cited); arrest 29 May (some say 20 May).
- **Profitis Ilias flag-bearer (1897)** and the **Splantzia hanging tree**: legend or tradition. Tell them as such.
- **"Only building in Greece with both minaret and bell tower":** widely repeated, not independently verified.
